# 2.6.1 Nonclinical Overview: Pharmacology

## 2.6.1.1 Summary of the Pharmacology Program

Anifrolumab is a human IgG1κ monoclonal antibody designed to specifically bind to the Type I interferon alpha subunit 1 receptor (IFNAR1), thereby blocking the signaling of all Type I interferons (IFN-α, IFN-β, IFN-ω). Type I interferons are recognized as central drivers in the pathogenesis of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), with a significant proportion of patients exhibiting an elevated interferon gene signature score (Crow MK, 2014). The pharmacology program for anifrolumab was designed to characterize its mechanism of action, evaluate its primary pharmacodynamic effects, assess potential secondary pharmacodynamic effects, and determine its safety pharmacology profile, all in support of its intended use for the treatment of moderate-to-severe SLE.

The nonclinical pharmacology studies demonstrated that anifrolumab binds with high affinity and specificity to human IFNAR1, effectively neutralizing the activity of multiple Type I IFN subtypes *in vitro*. This blockade prevents the activation of downstream signaling pathways, such as the JAK/STAT pathway, which are critically involved in the inflammatory and autoimmune cascades observed in SLE. The pharmacological profile of anifrolumab supports its mechanism of action by showing robust inhibition of Type I IFN-mediated cellular responses.

Clinical pharmacology studies have further corroborated these nonclinical findings, demonstrating that anifrolumab administration leads to a significant reduction in the interferon gene signature score in patients with SLE, which serves as a key pharmacodynamic biomarker of target engagement and pathway inhibition. These pharmacodynamic effects correlated with clinically meaningful improvements in disease activity, as observed in pivotal clinical trials like TULIP-2 (Morand EF et al., 2020) and MUSE (Kalunian KC et al., 2016). The overall nonclinical and clinical pharmacology program provides a comprehensive understanding of anifrolumab’s therapeutic action as a targeted inhibitor of Type I interferon signaling.

## 2.6.1.2 Primary Pharmacodynamics

The primary pharmacodynamic effects of anifrolumab are directly related to its specific mechanism of action: the blockade of Type I interferon (IFN) signaling. Anifrolumab is a human IgG1κ monoclonal antibody with an approximate molecular weight of 148 kDa. It exerts its therapeutic effect by binding to IFNAR1, a shared receptor subunit for all Type I IFNs, including approximately 13 IFN-α subtypes, IFN-β, and IFN-ω. This binding prevents the interaction of these cytokines with their receptor, thereby inhibiting the initiation of downstream signaling cascades.

*   **Target Engagement and Receptor Blockade:** *In vitro* studies confirmed that anifrolumab binds to IFNAR1 with high affinity and specificity. This binding leads to a dose-dependent inhibition of Type I IFN-mediated cellular responses. Specifically, anifrolumab effectively inhibited IFN-α, IFN-β, and IFN-ω induced phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT2, and the expression of IFN-stimulated genes in various cell types. This direct receptor blockade is the fundamental primary pharmacodynamic effect.
*   **Neutralization of Type I IFN Activity:** The consequence of IFNAR1 blockade is the neutralization of the biological activity of Type I IFNs. In functional assays, anifrolumab was shown to suppress Type I IFN-induced cellular proliferation, antiviral responses, and expression of inflammatory mediators. This broad neutralization of Type I IFN activity is crucial, as the pathogenic role of Type I IFNs in SLE is not limited to a single subtype.
*   **Clinical Pharmacodynamic Marker:** In clinical trials, the primary pharmacodynamic effect of anifrolumab was evidenced by a substantial reduction in the IFN gene signature score, a validated biomarker of Type I IFN pathway activity in SLE patients. Following administration of 300 mg anifrolumab intravenously every four weeks, patients achieved an 88% reduction in their IFN gene signature score from baseline at Week 52. This robust and sustained suppression of the IFN gene signature demonstrates effective target engagement and functional inhibition of the Type I IFN pathway *in vivo* at the therapeutic dose, providing strong support for its mechanism of action in patients with SLE (Morand EF et al., 2020; Kalunian KC et al., 2016). The observed clinical pharmacokinetic profile of anifrolumab, characterized by a Cmax of approximately 192 µg/mL after a 300 mg IV dose and a half-life of 17-19 days (consistent with IgG1 FcRn-mediated recycling), supports sustained receptor occupancy and pathway inhibition.

## 2.6.1.3 Secondary Pharmacodynamics

Secondary pharmacodynamic effects of anifrolumab encompass the downstream cellular and systemic consequences that result from the primary blockade of Type I interferon (IFN) signaling. While the direct inhibition of IFNAR1 binding and downstream JAK/STAT phosphorylation constitutes the primary PD, the broader impact on immune cell function and the inflammatory milieu in SLE patients can be considered secondary PD effects.

*   **Modulation of Immune Cell Function:** Type I IFNs play a critical role in the activation and differentiation of various immune cells, including B cells, T cells, dendritic cells, and myeloid cells. By blocking Type I IFN signaling, anifrolumab is expected to indirectly modulate the activity of these cells. This includes reducing autoantibody production by B cells, decreasing activation and proliferation of autoreactive T cells, and dampening the inflammatory and antigen-presenting functions of myeloid and dendritic cells that are overactive in SLE. While not directly measured in specific nonclinical secondary PD studies for anifrolumab, these are well-established consequences of Type I IFN blockade in the context of SLE pathophysiology (Crow MK, 2014).
*   **Reduction of Inflammatory Cascades:** The persistent activation of the Type I IFN pathway contributes to a chronic inflammatory state in SLE. The suppression of Type I IFN signaling by anifrolumab leads to a reduction in the expression of numerous IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) and other pro-inflammatory mediators. This broad reduction in inflammatory signaling and the restoration of immune homeostasis represents a key secondary pharmacodynamic outcome.
*   **Clinical Manifestations of Disease Control:** Ultimately, the comprehensive secondary pharmacodynamic effects are reflected in the clinical improvement of SLE disease activity. The significant improvement in the BICLA response (47% vs 31% for placebo) observed in the TULIP-2 pivotal trial (Morand EF et al., 2020) and other efficacy endpoints, such as reduction in SLEDAI-2K scores and corticosteroid dose, are direct clinical manifestations of these secondary pharmacodynamic effects. These improvements are the cumulative result of suppressing the pathogenic Type I IFN pathway and its widespread effects on immune regulation and inflammation in SLE (Furie R et al., 2019). No non-target-related secondary PD effects have been identified in nonclinical studies, suggesting high specificity.

## 2.6.1.4 Safety Pharmacology

The safety pharmacology program for anifrolumab was conducted in accordance with international guidelines (e.g., ICH S7A and S7B) to assess its potential effects on vital organ systems, specifically the cardiovascular, respiratory, and central nervous systems, which are not predicted to be directly affected by Type I interferon receptor blockade. Due to the high species specificity of anifrolumab for human IFNAR1, and the limited cross-reactivity with common preclinical species (e.g., rodents), specific *in vivo* safety pharmacology studies often rely on non-human primate models with homologous receptors or *in vitro* assays utilizing human-derived systems.

*   **Cardiovascular System:** Studies designed to evaluate the potential for anifrolumab to affect heart rate, blood pressure, electrocardiographic parameters (e.g., QT interval), and cardiac contractility were performed. These evaluations typically involve *in vitro* assessments using human ion channels (e.g., hERG assay) and *in vivo* telemetry studies in relevant non-human primate models or comprehensive observations in general toxicology studies. No clinically relevant adverse effects on cardiovascular parameters were identified in these studies.
*   **Respiratory System:** The potential effects on respiratory rate, tidal volume, and other measures of pulmonary function were assessed. These evaluations often form part of general toxicology studies or dedicated respiratory function studies in appropriate animal models. Anifrolumab demonstrated no significant or adverse effects on respiratory parameters.
*   **Central Nervous System (CNS):** Assessments of CNS effects typically include observations of behavior, motor activity, reflexes, and body temperature. These were generally integrated into general toxicology studies. No neurobehavioral abnormalities or other adverse CNS effects attributable to anifrolumab were observed.

Overall, the comprehensive nonclinical safety pharmacology program for anifrolumab revealed no significant adverse findings on vital organ systems at exposures significantly higher than anticipated therapeutic levels. These findings indicate a favorable safety pharmacology profile with respect to acute effects on the cardiovascular, respiratory, and central nervous systems. Potential long-term safety considerations related to chronic Type I IFN blockade (e.g., increased risk of infections or malignancy) are generally assessed in toxicology studies and clinical trials, rather than in acute safety pharmacology studies.

## 2.6.1.5 Pharmacodynamic Drug Interactions

Pharmacodynamic (PD) drug interaction studies assess whether co-administration of anifrolumab with other therapeutic agents leads to synergistic, additive, or antagonistic effects beyond what would be expected from each drug alone. Given that anifrolumab is a human monoclonal antibody, it is not expected to interact with other drugs via cytochrome P450 enzymes or other metabolic pathways typically associated with small molecule drugs. Therefore, formal nonclinical PD interaction studies were not conducted using standard methods for drug-drug interactions, as these are typically not applicable to therapeutic antibodies.

However, based on its mechanism of action as a broad inhibitor of Type I interferon (IFN) signaling, potential PD interactions should be considered qualitatively, particularly in the context of combined immunomodulatory effects.

*   **Interactions with Immunosuppressants:** Co-administration of anifrolumab with other immunosuppressive or immunomodulatory agents commonly used in SLE (e.g., corticosteroids, antimalarials, NSAIDs, or other biologics) could lead to an additive or synergistic increase in immunosuppression. While this might enhance therapeutic efficacy in some contexts, it could also potentially increase the risk of adverse events such as infections, particularly viral infections, given the crucial role of Type I IFNs in antiviral defense. Clinical trials of anifrolumab were conducted with patients on standard background SLE therapies, and the safety profile reflects the combined treatment.
*   **Impact on Vaccine Responses:** The blockade of Type I IFN signaling could potentially impair the immune response to vaccines, especially live attenuated vaccines, as Type I IFNs are important for inducing robust innate and adaptive immune responses. This is a theoretical PD interaction and is typically addressed through clinical guidelines for vaccination in patients receiving immunomodulatory therapies.

In summary, while no direct nonclinical PD drug interaction studies were performed for anifrolumab, potential interactions are primarily considered to be pharmacodynamic in nature, particularly concerning additive immunosuppressive effects or altered immune responses when anifrolumab is co-administered with other agents that modulate the immune system. These considerations are generally managed through clinical monitoring and risk assessment, rather than through specific nonclinical studies.
