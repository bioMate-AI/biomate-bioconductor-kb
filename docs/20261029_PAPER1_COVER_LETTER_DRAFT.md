Please consider our manuscript, “A benchmark framework to guide automated cyclic peptide discovery”, for publication as a [confirmed article type] in [confirmed journal].

Reliable benchmarks are a foundation for building automated cyclic-peptide discovery platforms: they establish whether learning from new measurements improves the choice of subsequent experiments. This requires distinguishing model updating from the advantage of an initial predictor or close measured analogues, testing transfer across experimental sources, and calibrating the procedures used to detect dataset shortcuts. Our study addresses these challenges in cyclic-peptide permeability replay and binding benchmarks using frozen-model, one-shot, shuffled-feedback and retrieval controls, together with uncertainty over independent sources and families.

A full-pool permeability gain that was reproducible across initialisation seeds did not establish a gain across sources: the 47.3-hit loop-minus-frozen contrast had a source-cluster interval of −53.0 to 147.6. Under transductive source standardisation, this result identifies source-sensitive selection effects that inform prospective study design. A separate held-out-source ranking evaluation showed an improvement over the frozen model (Spearman correlation gain, 0.075; 95% source-bootstrap interval, 0.037–0.115), while the advantage over shuffled feedback remained unresolved. Separately, planted controls revealed excessive false alarms in an initial benchmark-validity gate. Calibration reduced observed false-alarm rates under the tested conditions and showed that censoring and assay type, rather than the primary compound-only controls, drove the measured-inactive binding verdict. Strong analogue-retrieval baselines also limited what could be attributed to additional learning in few-label evaluations.

The methodological contribution is an integrated benchmark framework joining feedback attribution, transfer testing across experimental sources and calibration of benchmark-validity decisions. Its originality lies in connecting these assessments within cyclic-peptide permeability and binding evaluations, including testing the validity gate itself on planted controls. This yields concrete evidence about which apparent advances are supported, which depend on the comparator or experimental unit, and how prospective validation should be designed. The manuscript reports the gate's failure and correction, distinguishes seed replication from independent-source inference, and preserves inconclusive findings. It does not claim a new model architecture, generated-compound discovery or prospective experimental validation.

Beyond cyclic peptides, the framework suggests how learning controls, independent experimental units and calibrated validity tests could inform other drug modalities and automated laboratory workflows. These broader applications are proposed extensions requiring task-specific validation.
