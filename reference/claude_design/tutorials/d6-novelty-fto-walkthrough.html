Is this molecule actually new — and is it clear of occupied space?

You have a shortlist of candidate compounds and a budget that only stretches to a few CRO orders. Two questions decide which ones advance: how novel is each molecule against known chemistry, and does it sit inside already-occupied, possibly patented space? The Compound Novelty Scorer answers both — a nearest-neighbour similarity search against a library of known compounds, plus an opt-in patent-presence screen — and it is positioned as a gate at the pre-CRO chokepoint. The problem this solves

A CRO order is expensive — you don't want to spend it on a molecule someone already owns.

By the time a candidate reaches a contract research organization for synthesis or assay, it has real cost attached. The worst outcome is paying to make a compound that turns out to be a near-duplicate of a known, possibly patented structure — chemistry that is neither novel enough to claim nor clear enough to pursue. That check has to happen before the order, not after.

The ideas you need first

Four ideas, and the whole method follows.

You don't need cheminformatics internals — just these four concepts, in order.

1 · FINGERPRINT

A molecule as a bit-vector

Morgan / E-C-F-P-four encodes each compound's local substructures as a fixed binary fingerprint — a compact, comparable signature of its chemistry.

2 · TANIMOTO

Similarity between two

The Tanimoto coefficient measures overlap between two fingerprints, from 0 (nothing shared) to 1 (identical). It is the ruler for "how alike are these molecules."

3 · NOVELTY

Far from everything known

Search a library of known compounds for the nearest neighbour. A low maximum similarity means nothing close exists — the compound is novel.

4 · OCCUPIED SPACE

Novel ≠ clear

A presence screen against patent sources asks a second question: is this chemistry already disclosed? Structural novelty and legal clearance are distinct.

The whole method is: fingerprint the query, find its nearest known neighbour by Tanimoto, call it novel or occupied against a cutoff, and — if asked — check whether it is already present in patent space.

Inputs · what you bring

The candidates to score, and the library of known chemistry to score them against.

The screen is comparative, so the inputs are the query compounds and a reference library — plus two knobs that set how strictly novelty is called and whether patents are checked.

QUERY_SMILES

The candidates

Your shortlist, each written as a S-M-I-L-E-S string (query_smiles, required). These are the molecules the gate decides on.

FPSIM2_DB

The known library

A mounted FPSim2 .h5 fingerprint library of known compounds — for example ChEMBL or BindingDB (fpsim2_db, required). Novelty is relative to what is in here.

CUTOFF

How strict is "novel"

novelty_tanimoto_cutoff defaults to 0.85: a compound is called NOVEL when its maximum Tanimoto similarity to the library falls below the cutoff.

CHECK_PATENTS

Opt-in presence screen

check_patents defaults to false. Turn it on to add a presence lookup against SureChEMBL / PubChem alongside the similarity call.

Required: query_smiles, fpsim2_db. Optional: novelty_tanimoto_cutoff (default 0.85), check_patents (default false).

The method · what it actually computes

Fingerprint, nearest-neighbour search, then an optional patent-presence lookup.

The steps are concrete and sequential. The similarity search is the core; the patent screen is opt-in; and the engine is built to fail open — an unreachable source never silently blocks a candidate.

1 · FINGERPRINT

Morgan / ECFP4

Each query_smiles is encoded as a Morgan / E-C-F-P-four fingerprint — the same representation the library in fpsim2_db is built on, so the two are directly comparable.

2 · NEAREST NEIGHBOUR

Max Tanimoto vs the library

FPSim2 computes the Tanimoto similarity of the query against every compound in the library and returns the maximum — the single closest known neighbour and its score.

3 · PATENT SCREEN

Opt-in presence lookup

When check_patents is on, a presence check against SureChEMBL / PubChem asks whether the chemistry is already disclosed. Peptides use Needleman–Wunsch sequence identity instead.

Fail-open by design: if a data source is unreachable the engine does not silently block a candidate — it reports the gap rather than returning a false "clear."

The output · a verdict with its nearest neighbour

Max similarity, the closest known compound, and a novel-vs-occupied call.

For each candidate the screen reports the maximum Tanimoto similarity and the nearest known neighbour behind it, then the verdict against the cutoff. Below 0.85 the compound is called novel; at or above, occupied. With patents enabled, presence flags ride alongside.

NOVELTY RESULT

Score + nearest neighbour

The maximum Tanimoto similarity to the library and the single nearest known compound that produced it, with the novel-vs-occupied verdict read against novelty_tanimoto_cutoff. The neighbour is what makes the call inspectable.

PATENT FLAGS

If enabled

When check_patents is on, each candidate carries a patent-presence flag from the SureChEMBL / PubChem lookup — a signal that the chemistry is already disclosed somewhere.

  • The neighbour travels with the verdict. You see not just "occupied" but which known compound it is close to, so the call can be checked rather than taken on faith.
  • Wired into the pre-CRO gate. Candidates scored as occupied are dropped from the C-R-O request before ordering; only the novel, clear ones go forward.

Using the result · the pre-CRO gate

Drop the occupied candidates; send only the novel, clear ones to the CRO.

The screen earns its place by reshaping the CRO request at the chokepoint: candidates scored as occupied are removed before ordering, so budget goes to chemistry that is both novel and clear. It is a structural signal, so a flagged compound is a prompt to look, not an automatic rejection.

GATE

Screen before ordering

Every candidate runs through the Novelty Scorer before the C-R-O request is assembled. Occupied compounds are dropped from the order automatically.

PRIORITIZE

Spend where it is clear

The novel, un-flagged candidates carry forward. The CRO budget is committed to chemistry that is new and not obviously inside occupied space.

ESCALATE

Flagged → human review

A low-novelty or patent-flagged candidate goes to a patent attorney for the real freedom-to-operate call, not straight to the bin. The gate narrows; a person decides.

The gate is a filter on the CRO request, not a legal verdict: it removes the clearly occupied candidates cheaply and routes the borderline ones to expert review.

What you get, precisely

A structural screen, not a legal opinion — be exact about what it is.

The screen is fast and useful, and knowing exactly what it does not tell you is what keeps it trustworthy for a decision you will defend. A patent attorney, not a Tanimoto score, makes the freedom-to-operate call.

STRUCTURAL, NOT LEGAL

Similarity is a signal

Tanimoto novelty is a structural measure; the patent step is a presence SCREEN against SureChEMBL / PubChem, explicitly NOT a freedom-to-operate legal determination. A patent attorney makes the F-T-O call.

FINGERPRINT BLIND SPOTS

Scaffold-hops slip through

Fingerprint similarity can miss scaffold-hops and bioisosteres — compounds that are functionally related but structurally distant may read as novel when they are not meaningfully new.

COVERAGE & FAIL-OPEN

Absence is not proof

Results depend on the library's coverage, and fail-open means a candidate is never silently blocked — so absence of a hit is not proof of novelty, only that nothing close was found in the sources checked.

Specific and honest — a structural pre-screen with stated limits, not an FTO opinion
