The problem

A raw Cq is not an answer — and doing ΔΔCq by hand is fiddly.

A Cq of 24 for your gene means nothing on its own: it depends on how much sample went into the well, on pipetting, and on reaction efficiency. To say whether a gene went up or down, the numbers have to be normalised and compared.

1 · MANY SUBTRACTIONS

Every well, every gene

Each sample needs its target Cq minus its reference Cq, then a control average, then a power of two.

2 · EASY TO SLIP

One wrong cell

A mis-copied formula or a mislabelled control quietly shifts every fold-change that follows.

3 · HARD TO DEFEND

No clear record

A spreadsheet rarely shows which genes and which control group produced a given number.

The method itself is standard and well established. The goal is to apply it consistently, every time.

Background — the problem in one line
The ideas you need first

Four ideas that make a fold-change readable.

ΔΔCq is a short chain of three simple steps. These four ideas cover all of it.

1 · Cq VALUE

The raw number

The cycle where a well's signal crosses the line. Lower Cq means more starting material.

2 · REFERENCE GENES

The normaliser

Genes that should not change between conditions, such as GAPDH or ACTB. Subtracting them cancels out how much sample was loaded.

3 · ΔΔCq

Compared to control

Each sample's normalised value minus the control group's average. Zero means "same as control".

4 · FOLD-CHANGE

The readable answer

Two to the power of minus ΔΔCq: 2.0 is twice control, 0.5 is half. About one cycle is a two-fold change.

Treated versus control is the comparison. Fold-change above 1 means higher in treated; below 1 means lower; near 1 means unchanged.

The label that marks your control samples, so every treated sample can be compared against them.

Step 4 · Reading and trusting it

A standard method, a clear record, and a good habit.

The calculation is the published ΔΔCq method, applied the same way every run. What makes it defensible is what you can show alongside it.

  • Check the reference genes. A believable result has your reference gene looking flat across conditions. If it swings widely, the normalisation is shaky.
  • Replicates matter. A large fold-change from a single well is a hint; with three or more replicates per group it becomes a result.
  • A record you can cite. The reference genes, control label, software version, and output checksums are kept together as a reproducibility record.
  • Sanity-check your first run. On your own data, compare one fold-change with a value you calculate by hand. That is simply good practice, and it builds trust quickly.
