The problem this solves

Exposure is the first question in PK — and it has to be computed the standard way.

Every PK study starts with the same ask: summarise how much drug the body saw and how long it hung around. The numbers that answer it — AUC, Cmax, half-life, clearance — are standard, but they are easy to get subtly wrong: the choice of trapezoidal rule, how the terminal slope is fit, how much of the tail is extrapolated. Get those conventions wrong and the exposure estimate drifts, and the study is hard to defend.

HOW MUCH

Total exposure

The area under the concentration-time curve (AUC) — the single number that captures how much drug the body was exposed to.

HOW HIGH

Peak & timing

Cmax, the highest concentration, and Tmax, when it occurred — read straight off the observed points.

HOW LONG

Persistence

Terminal half-life — how fast the drug leaves once absorption is done. It sets the dosing interval.

HOW FAST

Clearance & volume

CL/F and Vd/F — the body's apparent rate of removal and apparent distribution space, derived from dose and AUC.

The ideas you need first

Four plain ideas are the whole of NCA.

You don't need a compartment model or a differential equation — just these four, read off the observed curve.

1 · THE CURVE

Concentration vs. time

The raw measurement: drug concentration at each sampling time, per subject. Everything else is derived from it.

2 · AUC

Area under the curve

Total exposure — the area under the concentration-time curve. AUClast to the last sample, AUCinf extrapolated to infinity.

3 · PEAK

Cmax & Tmax

The highest observed concentration and the time it happened — the shape of the absorption phase in two numbers.

4 · TAIL

Half-life & clearance

From the terminal slope: how quickly the drug clears, and the clearance and volume that follow from it.

NCA is model-independent: it summarises the observed data by established conventions, rather than fitting a mechanistic model. That is its strength — and its boundary, which we come back to at the end.

The input · the concentration-time table

A tidy concentration-time table, plus the dose and route.

NCA needs the observed curve in a defined shape. The first step validates it before any computation — so a malformed table fails fast and honestly, rather than producing a quiet, wrong answer.

REQUIRED COLUMNS

What each row carries

One row per sample: subject_id, time, conc, dose, route. The validation step checks these are present and the values are plausible.

DOSE & ROUTE

The study design

dose (default 100) and route — oral or iv. Route maps to PKNCA's extravascular vs. intravascular, which governs which parameters are defined.

UNITS

Labelled, not guessed

time_unit (default h) and conc_unit (default ng/mL) label the axes and the derived AUC, clearance and volume units in the report.

Step 1, NCA_INPUT_VALIDATION, emits a validated, normalised CSV and a validation report — negative concentrations are floored to zero, negative times are rejected.

The curve is checked before it is analysed
The computation · PKNCA, per subject

The standard exposure parameters, computed by convention — per subject.

The core step runs R/PKNCA over the validated curve and computes the standard NCA parameter set for each subject, using the linear-up / log-down trapezoidal method. These are the numbers a PK report is built from — all derived from the observed data, none fitted to a model.

  • Per-subject results land in nca_results.csv; population statistics (mean, SD, CV%) in nca_summary.json.
  • Method is stated: linear-up log-down trapezoidal, with the PKNCA version recorded for provenance.
  • Model-independent. Every parameter is a summary of the observed curve — nothing is fit to a compartment model.
  • CL/F and Vd/F are apparent for extravascular dosing — absolute bioavailability needs an IV reference.

In context · from exposure to effect

Exposure is half the story — the other half is what it does.

NCA tells you how much drug was present. To close the loop you ask how that exposure translates into effect. BioMate has companion workflows for exactly that step, so the exposure numbers from NCA feed a defensible dose rationale rather than sitting alone.

DOSE RESPONSE

Effect vs. concentration

The Dose Response workflow fits a 4- or 5-parameter log-logistic curve (LL.4 / LL.5) to concentration-effect data, estimating IC50 / EC50 and the Hill slope.

EXPOSURE–RESPONSE

AUC vs. outcome

The Exposure Response workflows fit sigmoid Emax / Hill models, linking an exposure metric — like the AUC NCA produced — to a measured effect.

THE CHAIN

Exposure → effect → dose

Observed curve → NCA exposure → exposure-response → a dose that puts exposure where the effect is. Each link is a real BioMate workflow.

These are separate workflows you route to next — NCA produces the exposure inputs they consume; it does not itself model the effect.

Interpretation · what each number means for dosing

Reading the parameters: each one answers a dosing question.

The report (nca_report.html) lays the parameters out with a log-linear concentration-time plot, a per-subject table, and a stated assumptions section. Here is how to read the headline numbers.

AUC

Is exposure adequate?

Compare AUC to a target. Too low and the drug under-exposes; too high and you risk toxicity. It also tests dose-proportionality across dose levels.

Cmax / Tmax

How fast, how high?

A high, early Cmax means rapid absorption and a sharp peak — relevant to peak-driven effect or toxicity. A late Tmax suggests slow or extended-release absorption.

t½

How often to dose?

Terminal half-life sets the dosing interval and how long until steady state. It is only as good as the terminal sampling that supports the slope.

CL/F · Vd/F

Scaling the dose

Clearance drives the maintenance dose for a target exposure; apparent volume relates the dose to the concentration it produces.

What NCA does — and doesn't — tell you

The honest edges: NCA summarises data; it does not model mechanism.

NCA is trustworthy precisely because its scope is narrow and explicit. The pipeline states its own assumptions and runs quality gates — and knowing where it stops is what tells you when to reach for a mechanistic model instead.

SAMPLING-DEPENDENT

The schedule is the ceiling

Tmax can only be a sampled time point; half-life needs 3+ terminal points to fit the slope. Sparse or short sampling limits what NCA can report — the pipeline flags subjects with no terminal slope.

EXTRAPOLATION

A watched quantity

AUCinf relies on extrapolating the tail. A QC gate checks the extrapolated fraction stays within 20% (FDA BA/BE convention); a large extrapolation makes AUCinf unreliable.

NCA vs. PBPK

Summary vs. mechanism

NCA won't predict a new dose, a drug interaction, or a special population. For that you need a mechanistic model — see S2, PBPK, which BioMate also runs.

Stated in the report's assumptions: linear PK, single-dose design, adequate terminal sampling, terminal log-linearity. NCA also does not detect flip-flop kinetics. No result values are invented — they come from your curve.

A specific, honest tool — a data summary, not a mechanistic model
