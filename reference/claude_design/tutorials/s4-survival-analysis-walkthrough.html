The problem this solves

Censoring breaks ordinary statistics — and it's in almost every study.

In time-to-event data, you rarely see the event for everyone. A study ends; a patient moves away; some are simply still alive. Those observations are censored — you know they survived at least this long, but not their full time. Treat the data as if the numbers were complete, and you throw that information away or quietly bias the answer.

THE WRONG TOOL · A

A t-test on "time"

Averaging survival times silently assumes everyone's event was observed. Censored patients have no final time to average — so the mean is wrong.

THE WRONG TOOL · B

Logistic "dead by now?"

Collapsing to a yes/no outcome at one moment discards when things happened, and depends entirely on an arbitrary cut-off.

THE RIGHT TOOL

Survival methods

Kaplan–Meier and Cox are built for censoring: a patient contributes information up to their last known time, and no further.

The need: use every patient's follow-up honestly — including the ones without an observed event
The ideas you need first

Four plain ideas carry the whole analysis.

You don't need the mathematics — just these four, and everything that follows reads clearly.

1 · TIME & CENSORING

Two columns

Each subject has a time (how long observed) and a status — 1 if the event happened, 0 if censored. That pair is the whole input.

2 · KAPLAN–MEIER

The survival curve

A step curve estimating the probability of surviving past each time, using censored subjects correctly up to their last visit.

3 · LOG-RANK TEST

Do groups differ?

A formal test of whether two or more survival curves — say treated vs control — differ more than chance would explain.

4 · HAZARD RATIO

By how much?

From a Cox model: the relative rate of the event per unit of a covariate. HR < 1 is protective, HR > 1 is harmful.

The arc is: describe survival (Kaplan–Meier) → compare groups (log-rank) → quantify and adjust for covariates (Cox hazard ratios).

Stage 1, the input · what a survival table needs

Three kinds of column: the time, the event, and what you compare by.

The input is an ordinary table — one row per subject. What makes it a survival table is a time column, an event-status column, and a grouping or covariate column. BioMate maps your headers to these roles and standardizes the status to the required 1/0 coding.

TIME

How long observed

Months, days, or years until the event or the last contact — e.g. a time column. The duration each subject was followed.

STATUS

Event or censored

A status column coded 1 = event (death, relapse), 0 = censored. BioMate maps common labels like "dead"/"alive" onto this coding.

GROUP / COVARIATES

What you compare by

A treatment arm or subgroup for Kaplan–Meier; any mix of clinical covariates — age, stage, markers — for Cox.

Grounded in the real workflow: the KM step fits survfit(Surv(time, status) ~ group); the Cox step fits coxph(Surv(time, status) ~ .) over the covariate columns.

A plain table becomes a valid survival input
Stage 2 · Kaplan–Meier + log-rank

Describe survival, then test whether the groups really differ.

The Kaplan–Meier workflow estimates the survival curve for each group and reports the numbers behind it. It is a descriptive step: it shows the shape of survival over time and the median survival per group — it does not, on its own, prove a cause.

THE CURVES

Survival over time

survfit() produces the step curve per group and saves it — the Kaplan–Meier plot plus a summary table of time, number at risk, and survival probability with confidence bounds.

MEDIAN SURVIVAL

The headline number

The time at which half the group has had the event — read directly from each curve, and comparable across groups.

LOG-RANK

Difference, or chance?

The log-rank test asks whether the separation between curves is more than sampling noise. A small p-value says the groups' survival genuinely differs.

Real outputs: a Kaplan–Meier curve PDF, a per-time survival summary table, and the saved fit object — all from the R survival package.

Curves and median survival per group — a comparison, not yet a mechanism
Stage 3 · Cox regression & Lasso-Cox

Quantify the effect — and, with many covariates, select which ones matter.

Kaplan–Meier compares groups; Cox regression puts a number on it. The Cox proportional-hazards model estimates a hazard ratio for each covariate while adjusting for the others. When covariates are many — a gene-expression signature with hundreds of features — a regularized Lasso-Cox selects a sparse, prognostic subset.

COX MODEL

coxph(Surv ~ covariates)

Fits all covariates jointly and reports a hazard ratio with confidence interval and significance for each — the adjusted effect of one factor, holding the rest fixed.

LASSO-COX

Many covariates → a few

The rlassocox workflow applies an L1 penalty with cross-validation to pick the optimal lambda, then extracts the non-zero (active) prognostic features and their coefficients.

VALIDATE

Hold-out, not just fit

It splits train / test, fits on the training set, and predicts risk on the held-out set — because feature selection on high-dimensional data is easy to overfit.

Grounded: rlassocox takes a gene-expression matrix + a survival table (time, status) + a gene network, cross-validates lambda, and writes a prognostic_features.csv of selected genes with coefficients.

Hazard ratios for the effect; a validated, sparse signature when covariates are many
Reading the result

What a hazard ratio says — and what "significant" does and doesn't mean.

The output is only as good as your reading of it. Two numbers carry most of the meaning: the hazard ratio and its confidence interval, together with the test's p-value.

THE DIRECTION

Above or below 1

HR = 1 means no effect on the event rate. HR < 1 means a lower hazard (protective); HR > 1 means a higher hazard (harmful). An HR of 0.70 is roughly a 30% lower rate.

THE UNCERTAINTY

The confidence interval

A 95% CI that crosses 1 means the effect is not distinguishable from "no effect" at this sample size — the point estimate alone can mislead.

SIGNIFICANCE

What p actually means

A small p-value says the difference is unlikely under "no difference". It does not measure effect size, prove causation, or guarantee the result replicates.

Read the HR, its interval, and the p-value together — never one alone
Reading the result so it holds up to review

The checks that make a survival result defensible.

These methods are standard and trustworthy, and BioMate runs the diagnostics that keep them so. Reading those checks — and the assumptions behind them — is what makes a survival result hold up to review.

ASSUMPTION

Proportional hazards

Cox assumes a covariate's effect is constant over time. The workflow runs the cox.zph() test and diagnostic plots — check them; if the assumption fails, the HR is hard to interpret.

DATA QUALITY

Censoring must be fair

Results assume censoring is unrelated to prognosis (non-informative). If sicker patients drop out, estimates are biased — no method repairs that.

SELECTION

Lasso picks are tentative

Lasso-Cox feature selection is data-dependent: a different sample can select different genes. Treat the signature as a hypothesis to validate, not a verdict.

INFERENCE

Association ≠ causation

A hazard ratio describes association, adjusted for the covariates you included. Unmeasured confounders remain; observational survival is not a causal claim.
