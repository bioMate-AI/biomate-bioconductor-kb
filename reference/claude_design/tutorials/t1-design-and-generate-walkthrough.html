The first step of drug discovery — before you make anything

Start a drug program from a filtered shortlist, not a blank page.

A new medicine usually begins as a single molecule that sticks to a disease-related target and changes what it does. Why this is hard · the problem it solves Concept

Too many possible molecules, and four demands at once.

The number of drug-like molecules is about 10⁶⁰ — more than the stars in the observable universe. No chemist can draw and test even a fraction by hand, and a useful drug has to satisfy several demands at the same time.

1 · POTENCY

Bind the target

Stick to the disease protein strongly enough to actually change what it does.

2 · SAFETY

Do no harm

Not damage the heart, the liver, and so on — the risks that end programs late.

3 · MAKEABILITY

Can be built

Synthesizable in a lab at reasonable cost — some look fine on paper but can't be made.

4 · BEHAVIOUR

Behaves in the body

Gets absorbed, and isn't cleared away too quickly.

Narrowing 10⁶⁰ down to a first testable shortlist takes a chemistry team many months. Design & Generate does that first filtering automatically — before you spend money making or testing anything.

The ideas you need first · generate, seed, SMILES Concept

You don't invent molecules — you give one seed, and BioMate varies it.

"Screening" tests molecules you already have; "generating" (this tool) creates new ones. The generator doesn't start from nothing — you give it a seed: one known molecule to build variations around, like handing a composer a melody.

SEED

One known molecule

Entered as a SMILES string — a standard text code for a structure. Aspirin is CC(=O)Oc1ccccc1C(=O)O.

WHERE TO GET ONE

Look it up — don't type it

Copy the "Canonical SMILES" from a free database like PubChem or ChEMBL, or ask your chemistry collaborators.

TARGET vs SEED

Protein vs molecule

Your target is a protein (e.g. JAK1). The seed is a small molecule. Have only the target? Look up a known binder and use its SMILES.

The starting point is a seed molecule: BioMate systematically enumerates chemically sensible variations around the seed you supply, then screens and ranks them — so every candidate is a deliberate, inspectable variation on a structure you chose, not a black-box guess.

How adventurous to be · the scaffold policy Concept

Keep the core, jump to a new one, or grow one up.

The scaffold is a molecule's core skeleton — the part that gives it its basic shape. You tell BioMate how far from the seed to roam.

STAY

Vary the decorations

Keep the same core, change only what's around it. Conservative — the safe default.

HOP

Switch the core

Move to a different core that should still fit the target — to avoid a competitor's patent, or escape a toxic core.

GROW

Build it up

Start from a small fragment and grow it into a full drug-sized molecule.

Why scaffold-hop in practice: a chemist often needs a molecule that works like the original but is legally and chemically distinct.

Background — one setting controls how far the search roams
The property window · a preference, not a rule Concept

Three numbers that predict whether it can be a pill.

Molecules that get absorbed tend to fall within certain ranges of three measurable properties. BioMate lets you set a target window — a soft preference used for ranking, not a strict cut-off. A promising molecule just outside it isn't removed; it simply scores slightly lower.

MW

Molecular weight

Roughly, the molecule's size.

logP

Oily vs water-loving

How well it dissolves in fat versus water.

TPSA

Polar surface area

How much of its surface is polar.

Together these predict whether a molecule can be an oral pill, how easily it crosses cell membranes, and whether it can reach targets inside the cell or the brain. Smaller and less-polar crosses more easily — so for an intracellular target, you might lower the window. (Peptides sit well outside this small-molecule window — which is why they're a separate modality.)

Soft preference — a molecule just outside is ranked lower, never deleted
Honest scoring · goals kept separate, safety visible Concept

No single magic score — and the safety gate never hides.

You want a molecule that is potent and safe and makeable. These goals pull against each other, so BioMate shows each one separately, in its own units, and never blends them into one "overall" number — a single blended score could let an unsafe molecule hide behind a good average. You decide how to weigh them.

MULTI-OBJECTIVE

Each goal, its own column

Potency, safety, makeability shown side by side — the trade-off is visible, not averaged away. You pick what matters most.

THE SAFETY GATE

Flagged, set aside — still shown

Known-risky substructures ("structural alerts" — e.g. groups linked to heart-rhythm or liver risk) are flagged with the reason and set aside, but never hidden. An alert is a caution flag, not a proven toxicity measurement.

An honest note on potency: predicting how strongly a molecule binds a specific target needs a model trained for that target. Where BioMate lacks a trustworthy one, it says "not available" rather than inventing a number. Today's scores are about drug-likeness and property fit, with safety as a gate.

Safety is a visible gate, not a secret filter — nothing hidden

Reading the results · the multi-objective view Live UILive product

Every candidate is one row; every goal keeps its own column.

Each row is a proposed molecule (BM-0001, BM-0002, …). Each goal has its own column — nothing is blended into one number. "score*" is only a ranking key, not a quality verdict. Verdict shows Accepted or Rejected, and rejected molecules stay visible with their reason. "why?" on each row explains the score, including the nearest known molecule it resembles.

Goals shown separately, rejected candidates shown with reason — nothing hidden
The trade-off chart · Pareto front & rejection summary Live UILive product

The best available compromises — and why some were set aside.

Each dot is a candidate; the dots along the front boundary are the best available compromises — to make one better on one goal, you'd accept it being worse on another. You pick from that boundary based on what matters most. The rejection summary tells you why candidates were dropped: a high rejection rate is the clearest signal that your seed or constraints need adjusting — a useful diagnostic, not a failure.

Rejections explained; empty-result cause named — actionable, not a dead end
What happens next · the closed loop Concept

A shortlist is a start — real lab results make the next round better.

These candidates are still a long way from the clinic; the value is starting smart. From the results you can export an order sheet — a file pairing each candidate with a lab ID so chemists can actually make and test the top molecules.

Design→ Make→ Test→ Learn→ Design again

When lab results come back they feed BioMate's Models module (Tutorial 2), which learns from the real measurements and proposes a better next round. That cycle — design → make → test → learn — is the closed loop, and it's how BioMate gets more accurate every round. Typical hand-offs: a clinician or biologist contributes the validated target and the real safety problems to avoid; BioMate produces and ranks candidates; a medicinal chemist reviews the shortlist and decides what to make.

Honest scope: today the small-molecule generator runs end-to-end. The Peptide and Protein modalities show the form and record your settings, but do not yet produce candidates — the screen says so.
