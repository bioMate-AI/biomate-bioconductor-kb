# M1 Search & evidence — asking "what is known?" — narration (EN)

## Step 1 — Title · a cited answer in seconds

Before any experiment, you need to know what is already established — is this target really linked to the disease, has this side effect been reported, what structures exist. BioMate answers that kind of question directly. You ask in plain language, and it reads the literature, the curated databases, and the patent record for you, then hands back a short answer where every claim carries the source it came from. The hour of manual literature review you'd normally spend becomes a cited answer in seconds — one you can open, check, and stand behind. Throughout, we'll follow one recognisable question: the evidence linking the protein JAK1 to rheumatoid arthritis.

---

## Step 2 — The problem this replaces

Answering "what is known" by hand is slow, and the result is hard to trust. You open a database, a search engine, a handful of papers, and you read — cross-checking as you go, and hoping you didn't miss the one study that disagrees. The evidence lives in different places, in different shapes, behind different search boxes: the literature in one, curated records in another, the patent record in a third. And when you're done, what you have is a summary without its sources — a claim a colleague has to take on your word, that you can't easily defend in a lab meeting, a grant, or a review. The real question is never just "what's the answer." It's "what's the answer, and how do I know it's right."

---

## Step 3 — How it works

BioMate turns that into a single step. You type your question the way you'd ask a colleague — "what is the evidence linking JAK1 to rheumatoid arthritis, and how strong is it?" — and BioMate reads your goal and answers it directly in the chat: a cited answer in seconds, with no pipeline to run and no compute to spend. To build it, BioMate draws on the published evidence across three kinds of source. The literature — peer-reviewed papers and preprints, from sources such as PubMed, synthesised with the disagreement left intact. The curated databases — target, structure, pathway, assay and trial records, from sources such as Open Targets. And the patent filings, read alongside the science so the answer reflects the full published picture. You don't choose where to look; you ask plainly, and the answer tells you what it drew on.

---

## Step 4 — What a good answer looks like

The answer that comes back is not a chatbot paragraph — it's a small sourced report with a fixed shape. The claims come first, and each factual sentence carries a little numbered marker that ties it to a source below; a sentence with no marker is context, written to read that way, never a cited fact in disguise. Beneath the claims is the source list — one row per source, each showing the paper's title, its PubMed ID, and a link that opens it at the publisher for the full text. And the panel shows how the answer was searched: the databases it drew on — Europe PMC, OpenAlex, PubMed — with how many results each returned and how many were kept, so the retrieval behind the answer is visible, not hidden.

---

## Step 5 — One message box, two kinds of request

It helps to know where this sits. Every BioMate request is handled in one of two ways, and you ask for both the same way, from the same message box. Search and evidence always comes back as a sourced answer in the chat, returned in seconds, reading nothing of yours and starting no computation — what you use to settle a question before you commit time or money. The other kind runs a full analysis workflow on your own files, on cloud computers, with one report back — that's the next module. You never have to choose between them yourself; BioMate reads which one your question needs and tells you up front — shown as "Answer here, with sources" — and it always confirms before it starts a run in the cloud.

## Step 7 — How to trust it

That trust is built into the shape of the answer, not bolted on. Every claim is tied to a source that actually exists and that you can open: BioMate reports what it found, and it does not invent references. It answers "what is known" — it synthesises the published record and carries its sources with it, so the conclusion and its evidence always travel together. The boundary block is always shown: a short note saying what the answer is not evidence for — here, that it synthesises published literature and is not a verdict on your own data, which you'd test with a Cloud run. And where the base is thin — one source, or one lab — the answer says so plainly, rather than dressing a weak claim in confident prose. A hedged answer is the honesty working: a claim you can weigh, not one you have to take on faith.

So the habit is simple, and the same every time. Ask a plain-English evidence question; read the answer, with its claims and their numbered sources; open a citation to verify it at the source; read the boundary note; then carry the finding forward. That's the whole value in one place — an hour of manual literature review becomes a cited answer in seconds, drawn from real literature, databases, and patents, with its provenance attached. And because the sources travel with the answer, it's something you can paste into a lab meeting, a grant, or a review, and genuinely stand behind — a cited answer you can trust and trace.
